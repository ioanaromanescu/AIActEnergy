# CARD 03 — Generative AI assistants on engineering and HSE documents

*AI in Energy Operations: An EU AI Act Guide · [all cards](../README.md) · a guide and general information, not legal advice*

## What it is

Chat assistants built over an organisation's own documentation: decades of well reports, technical standards, operating procedures, permits, and incident databases. Typical uses are answering procedural questions, summarising standards, locating precedent in old reports, and drafting first versions of routine documents. Several operators have publicised in-house models and assistants of this kind.

## AI Act status

**Usually outside the high-risk categories, with a transparency duty and a supply chain to check.**

- Searching and summarising an organisation's own technical documents does not correspond to an Annex III category, so the high-risk regime will not normally apply.
- **Article 50 applies from August 2026**: people must be informed they are interacting with an AI system where that is not obvious, and content generated by the system must be marked as artificially generated in machine-readable form.
- The **general-purpose AI layer sits upstream**. Since August 2025, providers of general-purpose models carry their own obligations, including technical documentation, information for downstream providers who build on the model, a copyright policy, and a public summary of training content. An organisation deploying an assistant is relying on that chain, which is a reason to ask the vendor what it can show.
- The category can move if the same assistant is pointed at a different job. An assistant that begins with engineering documents and ends up screening people or informing decisions about workers is in Annex III point 4 territory, which is a different conversation entirely.

## If you deploy it

- Say clearly, in the interface, that this is an AI assistant.
- Article 4 does the heavy lifting here. The people using it need a realistic picture of how a language model fails: fluently, confidently, and most often on the detail that matters.
- Write down which questions the tool may be used for and which it may not, with safety-critical procedures on the explicit list.
- Keep a human between the assistant and anything that becomes a permit, a procedure, or an instruction to a crew.
- Ask the vendor for the upstream documentation, and read what the terms say about whether prompts and documents are used to train anything.

## GDPR overlay

**Medium.** The documents are technical, but incident reports, permits, and HSE records name people and describe what happened to them, sometimes including health information. Prompts and outputs are usually processed by the vendor and often logged. The practical checklist is a lawful basis for the underlying records, a processor agreement covering the assistant, clarity on training use, retention limits on prompt logs, and a look at where the processing physically happens.

## Red flags

- A confidently invented clause from a standard, pasted into a procedure by someone who had no reason to doubt it.
- Scope creep from documents to people.
- Answers quoted as authority in meetings, with nobody opening the source.
- Vendor terms silent on whether prompts and uploaded documents train the model.
- No record of what the assistant has been asked, which makes any later review impossible.
